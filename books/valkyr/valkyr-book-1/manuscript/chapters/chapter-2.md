# Chapter Two

Richard Jameson had the kind of face that made people want to trust him, and he’d clearly known that about himself for a very long time.

Vexx met him three weeks into his own re-entry, in a briefing room two levels below anything with a name on the door — the kind of space that existed on no building schematic, accessible by an elevator that required a code nobody wrote down. Jameson was already seated when Vexx arrived, going through a data pad with the unhurried attention of a man who had never once in his career been kept waiting. Fifties, maybe older — it was hard to tell with the kind of longevity treatments senior ONI officers had access to — silver at the temples in a way that read as distinguished rather than old, a UNSC dress uniform worn like it had been tailored that morning instead of issued a decade ago.

He stood when Vexx entered. That alone told Vexx something — commanding officers didn’t stand for operators, not in his experience, not unless they wanted something from you or wanted you to believe they respected you enough that the difference wouldn’t matter.

“Vexxcerian.” Jameson said the name like he’d practiced it, gotten the weight of every syllable right. “I’ve read your file. All of it — the parts most people in this building aren’t cleared for.” He extended a hand. “I’m sorry it took me this long to meet you in person. I wanted to give you time to find your feet before I put my thumb on the scale.”

Vexx shook it. The grip was firm, warm, exactly as calibrated as everything else about him. “Sir.”

“Drop the *sir* when it’s just us,” Jameson said, waving him toward a chair. “You spent longer in this uniform than most of the people who’ll technically outrank you here. I’d rather earn the formality than have it handed to me by protocol.” A pause, and something in his expression shifted toward something softer, something that looked, as far as Vexx could tell, like real feeling. “I knew your brother’s file before I knew yours. What happened to him—” He stopped himself, shook his head once, the practiced restraint of a man who had delivered condolences before and knew better than to overdo them. “He was one of the best operators this program ever fielded. I want you to know that. Whatever else you’ve been told, whatever you haven’t — that part was never in question.”

It should have felt like nothing. A commanding officer saying the things commanding officers say. But Vexx had spent four years turning over an empty casket in his mind, hungry for anyone who’d say Rx’s name like it meant something, and Jameson said it exactly right — grave without performance, warm without excess.

Vexx didn’t answer right away. He looked at the table, at the grain of it, and waited until he was reasonably sure of his own voice. “Thank you, sir,” he said, and remembered, half a second too late, that he’d been asked not to.

He had no way of knowing, sitting in that chair, that the man across the table had watched his brother die at close range and personally written the report that buried it.

* * *

Rx said nothing during the meeting. Vexx noticed that only afterward, walking the length of a gray corridor back toward the elevator, running the conversation back in his head out of old habit — a debrief instinct that had never quite left him even in retirement.

*You were quiet in there.*

*Was I.* Rx’s voice, flat in a way that wasn’t quite his usual flat — not the processed neutrality of the interface, something else underneath it. *Didn’t have much to add. He did all the talking.*

*You always have something to add.*

*Not always.* A beat, half a second too long. *He seems fine, Vexx. Competent. You liked him — I could hear it in your voice. That’s good. It’s good you’ve got someone in this building who isn’t a stranger with a data pad.*

Vexx let it sit. It wasn’t a lie, exactly — nothing Rx said was ever technically a lie, not in those early months. It just wasn’t the whole of whatever had gone through him in that room, and Vexx, three weeks back into a life he barely recognized, didn’t have the instinct yet to go looking for the rest of it. He filed the quiet away as nothing. New voice, new body, new way of processing a room full of strangers. Rx would need time to recalibrate to people the way Vexx was still recalibrating to everything.

He didn’t know, that day, that somewhere in the deep, sealed-off part of Rx’s mind that no diagnostic could reach, a cursor had hitched on Jameson’s voice the way skin flinches from a burn it can’t consciously remember getting.

* * *

The assignment came two weeks later, delivered not by Jameson himself but by a mid-level liaison Vexx would come to know only as Voss — clipped, efficient, unbothered by anything short of a hull breach. *You’re building a cell*, she told him, sliding a data pad across a table not unlike the one in Jameson’s office, though several floors up and considerably less carefully lit. *Five operators, five AI. You’ll have final say on selection from a shortlist we’ve already screened. Command wants this cell operational within the quarter.*

*Why me?*

*Because you’re the only person in this building who’s already proven the pairing process can survive contact with something this unconventional.* Voss didn’t quite smile, but something close to it moved across her face. *Command’s betting the rest of the program can learn from what worked with you and your AI. You’re the proof of concept whether you like the phrase or not.*

Vexx didn’t much like the phrase. He took the data pad anyway.

The shortlist ran longer than he expected — dozens of names, dozens of donor profiles, dozens of half-built psychological assessments predicting compatibility scores between operator and prospective AI with a confidence Vexx found faintly obscene, given what he knew about how unpredictable the actual bonding process could be. He spent four days going through it properly, Rx running alongside him the whole time, cross-referencing service records, flagging inconsistencies, occasionally offering an opinion Vexx hadn’t asked for and was glad to get anyway.

*This one*, Rx said, on the second day, highlighting a service file that read, on paper, like nothing special — a big man, unremarkable rank, a demolitions specialty buried three pages deep in a file mostly concerned with disciplinary footnotes. *Trust me.*

*Why him?*

*Instinct.* A pause. *And because I read the donor file attached to his proposed AI pairing, and I think you’re going to want this one on the team before you even meet him.*

Vexx read it. A veteran, retired, demolitions and covert ops, deceased eighteen months prior, no family listed — except for a single addendum buried at the bottom of the donor consent form, handwritten in a place where donor forms weren’t supposed to have handwriting at all: *For whoever needs it. Keep them from the dark.*

He didn’t fully understand yet what he was looking at. He requested the file be moved to the top of his list anyway.

* * *

His name was Goliath, and the file did not do him justice.

He filled the doorway of the assessment room the way weather fills a sky — not aggressive about it, just total, unavoidable, the kind of presence that made the room feel smaller the moment he stepped into it. Six foot seven without the armor, built like something designed rather than born, with a handshake that could have crushed stone and chose, deliberately, not to.

“Heard you’re putting a team together,” Goliath said, dropping into a chair that groaned audibly under him. “Heard you’re doing the choosing yourself instead of letting command hand you a roster.”

“That’s the idea.”

“Good.” He said it simply, like it settled something. “I’ve had enough of being handed to people.”

Vexx liked him immediately, in the uncomplicated way you like someone who says exactly what they mean and doesn’t dress it up first. He asked about the demolitions record, the disciplinary footnotes, and got back a story about a commanding officer who’d ordered a strike on a position with civilians still unaccounted for, and a young NCO who’d refused to detonate until the count came back clean, and the eleven-month black mark on his record that decision had cost him.

“Would you do it again?” Vexx asked.

“Every time,” Goliath said, without a flicker of hesitation. “Man who taught me demolitions used to say there’s a difference between clearing a threat and just clearing a problem. I don’t do the second one. Never have.”

Vexx thought about the handwritten line on the donor form. *Keep them from the dark.* He didn’t mention it. He didn’t need to. He’d already decided.

The pairing process, when it happened three days later, went smoother than Vexx’s own had — no four-month gap of grief first, no gray room and a stranger delivering impossible news. Just Goliath, sitting in a chair not unlike the one Vexx had sat in, and a voice coming online in his ear for the first time.

*You’re bigger than I expected*, the AI said, and Goliath actually laughed — a real, surprised bark of a laugh that filled the whole room.

“That’s Spector,” Vexx told him, watching the readouts stabilize faster than any diagnostic Vexx had seen run. “Should’ve mentioned. He doesn’t waste time with small talk.”

“I like him already,” Goliath said.

Vexx didn’t tell him, not that day, what he’d read in the donor consent form — the demolitions veteran with no family listed, who’d spent his last certain hours thinking not about himself but about a stranger he’d never meet, someone who might one day need keeping from the dark. That story belonged to Goliath to find on his own time, in his own way. Vexx had a feeling, watching the two of them settle into an easy rhythm within the first hour, that it wouldn’t take long.

He was right, though it took longer than an hour — three weeks, in fact, most of them spent on a training range Vexx had commandeered for the cell’s early drills, the two of them running breach-and-clear scenarios until the walls of the mock structure were scorched black in a dozen places.

“You’ve got a rhythm,” Goliath said to Spector, toward the end of one such session, wiping sweat off his brow with the back of one enormous hand. “Set-charge-clear, set-charge-clear. Real specific cadence. Where’d you pick that up?”

“Comes with the donor stock,” Spector said, easy, unbothered. “He was thorough. Never left a breach half-finished in his life, way I understand it.”

“He got a name?”

A pause — longer than Spector’s pauses usually ran. “Phantom,” he said finally, like the word had weight he hadn’t expected to feel handing it over. “Least, that’s what people called him. I don’t actually know if that was ever official.”

Goliath went very still.

“You alright?” Spector asked, picking up on it immediately, the way he seemed to pick up on most things about his operator faster than made strict logical sense for six weeks of acquaintance.

“There was a man,” Goliath said slowly, like he was testing the memory for solidity before he trusted it out loud, “showed up on my homeworld when I was a kid. Colony under threat — pirates, insurrectionists, I never got the full story, I was too young to be told. One Spartan came in, ended it clean, quiet, gone before the local news crews even got a camera up. Nobody in town ever got a name. Just called him what he clearly wanted to be called. Ghost. Phantom. Something like that.”

Neither of them said anything for a long moment. On the range around them, the scorch marks from the last drill were still cooling, faint threads of smoke rising into the overhead lights.

“Small galaxy,” Spector said finally, careful, quiet — the voice of someone laying a stone down gently rather than dropping it.

“Yeah,” Goliath said. He didn’t sound entirely convinced it was coincidence. Vexx, watching from the observation deck above the range, didn’t hear the exchange in full — only saw Goliath go quiet in a way that didn’t suit him, and Spector’s readouts hold unusually steady beside him, like an AI being very careful not to say more than he already had.

Vexx made a note to ask about it later. Like so much else that autumn, he didn’t get around to it before the next pairing pulled his attention elsewhere.

* * *

The rest of the roster came together over the following six weeks, each pairing its own small negotiation between instinct and file review, Rx weighing in more on some than others.

Gaia arrived already half-formed as a soldier before Vexx ever met her — a recon specialist with a service history built almost entirely around solo operations, someone who’d survived three deployments other operators in her cohort hadn’t, largely, Vexx suspected, by trusting absolutely no one but herself. She was suspicious of the whole selection process in a way she didn’t bother hiding, sitting across from Vexx with her arms crossed and her chair angled toward the door.

“Command doesn’t build cells like this out of charity,” she said. “What’s the real reason I’m here?”

“Because you’re good,” Vexx said. “And because I think you’ve spent long enough doing this alone.”

“I like alone.”

“I know. I read your file.” He held her gaze. “I’m not going to pretend I can talk you out of that instinct. I’m asking if you’ll try something else anyway.”

She didn’t answer that day. She stood, pushed the chair back to exactly the angle she’d found it at, and left — and stopped in the doorway with one hand on the frame. “If I say yes and you start managing me,” she said, without turning around, “I’ll tell you once. After that I’ll just stop telling you things.”

She came back four days later and asked when the pairing process started, which Vexx took, correctly, as her answer. Her AI — Echo — came online talking, mid-sentence, as though he’d been narrating the room before anyone gave him a voice to do it out loud, and didn’t stop for the better part of an hour. Gaia looked, for the first ten minutes, deeply unsure she’d made the right call. By the end of the hour, something in her posture had eased in a way Vexx recognized, because he’d felt it himself in a gray room four years into a grief he hadn’t known how to carry alone.

Paladin came next — steady, broad-shouldered, the kind of soldier other soldiers instinctively formed up behind without being told to. His file was full of commendations for actions that boiled down, again and again, to the same instinct: covering a retreat, buying time for an evac, staying in a position longer than regulation strictly called for so that someone else could get out. His AI, Bastion, carried that same instinct forward from a donor who’d died doing exactly that — a combat medic who’d stayed behind to buy an evac window and hadn’t made it through it himself.

“Nobody gets left,” Paladin said, in his first sit-down with Vexx, like it was a fact of the universe rather than a personal creed. “That’s the only rule I’ve ever needed.”

Vexx told him the truth about the posting, or as much of it as he was cleared to tell — that the cell would sometimes be sent to look at its own people, and that looking was not always where it ended. Paladin heard him out. Didn’t interrupt, didn’t argue, sat with his hands folded on the table like a man in a chapel.

“You’re saying there’ll be days the order is to leave somebody,” he said. “And you’re saying you’d rather it was me standing there than somebody who wouldn’t mind. I understand you.” He reached across and moved the data pad out from under Vexx’s elbow, and then apologized for reaching. “That’s a yes. I’d just like it written down somewhere that I said no first.”

Vexx wrote his name on the roster and didn’t say what he was thinking — that a rule like that was going to run headlong, someday, into exactly the kind of order this program existed to give.

Zeus was harder. His file carried a weight the others didn’t — years spent attached to ONI intelligence units doing work that didn’t have a clean name, interrogation methods flagged more than once by internal review and never actually stopped. Vexx sat with that file longer than any other, turning it over, weighing what it would mean to bring someone like that into a program he was trying, in some inarticulate way he hadn’t fully admitted to himself yet, to build cleaner than the one that had killed his brother.

*You’re hesitating*, Rx said, watching him read the file for the third time.

*He’s good at things I don’t want us to need.*

*That’s exactly why you need him*, Rx said, quiet, certain. *You want to build something clean, Vexx. Clean doesn’t mean soft. It means knowing exactly what you’re capable of and choosing, every time, not to become it by accident. You need someone on this team who already knows where that line is, because he’s crossed it and lived with what’s on the other side.*

Vexx thought about that for a long time. He thought about his brother, dead behind a door marked classified, and wondered, not for the first time, what line Rx had refused to cross that had gotten him killed for it.

He asked for the interview the next morning.

Zeus was already in the assessment room when Vexx came in, sitting at the far end of the table rather than across from it, hands flat on the surface, nothing in front of him — no data pad, no file, none of the small props men bring into a room where they expect to be weighed. He didn’t stand.

“You’ve read it,” he said.

“I’ve read it.”

“Then you already know the parts I’d tell you.” He tipped his head a fraction, in no hurry at all. “How long have you been out?”

“Four years.”

“Sleeping through the night yet?”

It was such a small question, and so entirely beside the file lying between them, that Vexx answered it before he’d decided to. “No.”

“No.” Zeus gave the word back with no weight on it at all — not sympathy, not agreement, just the sound of it returned so Vexx could hear what he’d said. Then he moved on.

“Internal review flagged you three times,” Vexx said. “Nobody stopped you.”

“That’s right.”

“I’d like your side of it.”

“I don’t have a side.” He said it the way another man might report the weather. “A side is something you build when you want to keep something. I’m not trying to keep anything.”

Vexx waited. Zeus let the silence run, unhurried, comfortable in it in a way almost nobody is, and after a while it became apparent that he was prepared to let it run all morning.

“You want to know if I enjoyed it,” Zeus said eventually.

“That wasn’t the question.”

“It’s the question under the question. You’re building something you’ve decided is going to be a certain way. You’re not worried about what I did — you’ve read what I did, and you came anyway. You’re worried about what I am, because if I’m the second thing, then bringing me in makes you the man who brought me in.” Nothing in his face had moved since Vexx came in. “I’m not going to reassure you about that. I’d only be doing it to make the room easier, and then you’d have hired me for the wrong reason.”

Vexx sat with that a moment. Outside in the corridor somebody wheeled a cart past, badly, one wheel dragging.

“Say the day comes when a brief says *assess* and means something else,” he said. “What do you do.”

“Depends who’s in the room.” Zeus turned one hand over on the table, palm up, and put it back. “If you were going to end a man, you’d want it to be a decision. Most of the time it isn’t. Most of the time it’s four people not saying anything and a fifth one filling the silence, and afterward everybody’s account of it is a little different, and nobody lied.” A beat. “You don’t need me for the ending. You need me for the twenty minutes before it, while it’s still possible for it not to be one.”

“That’s not what your file says you’re good at.”

“My file was written by people who only ever sent for me at the end.”

Vexx looked at him for a while. “Why do you want this.”

“You’re the one who signs it,” Zeus said. “Not command. You. That’s the only unusual thing about this posting and it’s the only part I care about.” He stood, then, and the height of him came as a small surprise — he had made himself smaller in the chair and Vexx hadn’t seen him do it. “You didn’t ask me whether I’d do it again. Everybody asks that. I’d put money on Goliath getting asked that.”

“Would you do it again?”

“That’s not a question I can answer honestly, and you’d know I was lying, and we’d both have wasted a morning.” He pushed his chair back under the table, a small tidy careful movement, and squared it. “Ask me what I’d need in order not to.”

Vexx asked.

“Somebody in the room who’d tell me no,” Zeus said. “And be listened to.”

He signed Zeus’s transfer request the same afternoon.

His AI, Hollow, spoke less than any of the others in that first pairing session — precise, watchful, giving away nothing until it was useful to give it away. Zeus seemed unbothered by the quiet. If anything, he seemed to prefer it.

* * *

The last pairing was the one Vexx almost missed — a name buried near the bottom of the shortlist, easy to overlook next to louder service records. Aglaope. A Spartan who’d spent her final active tour doing something the file struggled to categorize cleanly: extraction and last-contact work, pulling survivors out of collapsed positions, and — when she couldn’t save someone — simply staying. Long enough that whoever was dying didn’t do it alone.

Vexx read that file twice before he understood why it had stopped him.

He met her in person the following week — quiet in a way that wasn’t shyness, watchful in a way that wasn’t suspicion, like someone who had spent so long being the last voice in a dying person’s ear that she’d learned to listen for what people actually needed instead of what they said. She didn’t ask him the questions Gaia had asked. She just listened to him explain the cell, the program, the shape of what he was trying to build, and said, when he finished, “You’re carrying something. I don’t need to know what it is. I just want you to know I noticed.”

It was such a simple thing to say. It undid him a little, in a way none of the other interviews had.

Her AI, when the pairing happened, spoke last of all six, and softest. *Requiem*, the voice said, when Vexx asked. *Feels right, doesn’t it. A song for the ones who don’t get to finish theirs.* And then, quieter, almost an aside: *I intend to keep singing it for people, not just against them. I hope that’s what you’re building here.*

Vexx didn’t have an answer for that yet. He hoped it was true. He was starting to understand, six pairings into a roster he’d built with his own hands instead of one handed to him, that hope was doing an enormous amount of work in every decision he’d made since the gray room — hope that this program could be different from the one that had killed his brother, hope that Jameson’s kindness in that first meeting was as real as it had felt, hope that the small, unexplained silences in Rx’s voice were nothing more than a mind still learning its own new shape.

He would spend the better part of two years finding out how much of that hope had been earned, and how much of it had simply been the only thing keeping him upright.

* * *

They had three days between the last pairing and the first deployment, and Vexx used them, deliberately, to put the six operators in a room together with nothing to do but be people instead of assets.

It went better than he expected. Goliath and Paladin took to each other immediately, two big men trading stories about basic training instructors who’d made their lives miserable, laughing loud enough that Zeus, sitting apart at the end of the table, allowed himself something that might, in poor lighting, have passed for a smile. Aglaope sat near Gaia without being asked to, and though Gaia didn’t warm to her the way Vexx half-hoped she would, she didn’t pull away either — which, for Gaia, he was learning, counted as something close to an invitation.

“You run cold,” Gaia said to Vexx at one point, catching him alone near the coffee station, arms crossed the way they usually were when she was deciding whether to say something. “Command style, I mean. Careful. Deliberate. I’ve served under commanders like that before. Half of them got people killed being too careful at the wrong moment.”

“And the other half?”

“Kept people alive being careful at the right one.” She studied him a second longer. “I haven’t decided which kind you are yet.”

“Neither have I,” Vexx admitted, and something about the honesty of it seemed to land better with her than reassurance would have. She didn’t say anything else, just gave him a short nod and went back to her seat, but Rx, quiet in his ear, offered a small, approving hum that told Vexx he’d said the right thing without fully meaning to.

*She’s going to test you*, Rx said, once the room had thinned out for the night. *Every chance she gets, for a while yet.*

*I know.*

*Good. Means she cares whether you pass.*

Vexx thought about that walking back to his quarters that night — about a roster of six people who’d each, in their own way, been discarded or overlooked or handed a designation instead of a name, now sitting in a room together learning how to be a unit instead of six separate files. It was the first night since the empty casket that he’d gone to sleep without the apartment feeling like an outline with nothing in it.

* * *

The six of them stood together for the first time in a briefing room on a gray morning three days later — Goliath and Spector, Gaia and Echo, Paladin and Bastion, Zeus and Hollow, Aglaope and Requiem, and Vexx at the head of the table with Rx’s voice steady in his ear, the whole strange, cobbled- together shape of something that didn’t have a name he was allowed to say out loud yet, but felt, for the first time since a folder full of nothing had been handed to him over a funeral he shouldn’t have had to attend, like it might actually hold.

Jameson delivered the mission brief himself. Vexx would remember that detail later, turning it over in the dark the way he turned over everything eventually — that Jameson hadn’t needed to be there, that a man with his rank rarely showed up in person for a first deployment briefing of a cell this small, and that he had anyway, standing at the front of the room with that same practiced warmth, welcoming six operators and six AI into a program he’d built his entire career on top of a lie about.

“You’re the first full cell this rebuilt program has fielded,” Jameson told them, and something in his voice, to every ear in the room but one, sounded exactly like pride. “I want you to understand what that means. Whatever this becomes — it starts here. With you.”

Rx said nothing again. Vexx noticed it again, and again let it go.

Somewhere behind six sets of eyes, six AI ran quiet diagnostics on a room they’d just met, cataloguing nothing in particular, waiting for the mission details that would send them, for the first time, into the field together.

Vexx looked at his team. Looked, for half a second too long, at Jameson.

And felt, without understanding why, the faintest, coldest thread of unease move through him — gone before he could name it, filed away, as nothing.
