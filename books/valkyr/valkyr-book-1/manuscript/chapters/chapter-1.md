# Chapter One

The knee had been the excuse. Everyone on the review board knew it, and everyone let him have it anyway, because the alternative was writing down the real reason in a permanent record, and nobody wanted that particular sentence attached to a Spartan’s file.

*Subject can no longer distinguish combat from memory.*

Vexx had rehearsed the knee story in his head for a week before the hearing — the way the joint locked on hard landings, the half-second lag between decision and execution that could get a man killed or, worse, get the men around him killed. All true. None of it the reason he was sitting in a gray room requesting his own discharge from the only life he’d known since he was six years old.

The real reason had arrived three weeks earlier, at 0300, when he’d come up out of sleep with his sidearm already in both hands, sighted on his own bunk door, heart going like a hammer against a rail — and the door had opened, and it had been Rx, home on a forty-eight-hour rotation, raising both hands with that half-grin he always wore when he was about to make fun of something Vexx didn’t want made fun of.

*Easy, killer. It’s just me.*

Vexx hadn’t lowered the weapon right away. That was the part that stayed with him. Some old, cold piece of his brain had needed a full two seconds to confirm that the shape in the doorway was his brother and not a threat, and in those two seconds he had genuinely, physically considered pulling the trigger.

He didn’t tell the review board that story. He told them about his knee. They let him go with the kind of quiet efficiency ONI reserved for assets that had become liabilities — no ceremony, no send-off, just a data pad to sign and a handshake from an officer who couldn’t meet his eyes for more than a second at a time. Vexx understood. A Spartan who no longer trusted his own hands wasn’t a soldier anymore. He was a very expensive rumor of one.

He remembered walking out of that building for the last time in a uniform that already felt like it belonged to someone else, the base noise around him — dropship engines spooling, drill instructors barking somewhere out past the motor pool, the particular clatter of a mess hall at 1100 — receding behind him like a tide going out. He’d expected relief. He got something closer to vertigo. Twenty-six years of his life had a shape, and the shape had just been surgically removed, and what was left was an outline with nothing in it.

Rx had shipped out three weeks before all of that, to somewhere the paperwork didn’t follow. Different program. Different everything, though Vexx hadn’t known that yet — only that his brother had started coming back from deployments with three redacted lines where the after-action report should be, and a status line that just read ACTIVE, like the war had swallowed the rest of the sentence and decided that was enough for anyone to know.

They’d grown up sharing everything, the two of them — a bunk, a birthday, a set of genetics some committee somewhere had decided were worth stealing a childhood over. Twins made for an efficient administrative shorthand in the program: same baseline, same augmentation windows, easier to track as a matched pair than as two separate variables. Vexx had hated that reasoning for most of his life and been grateful for it more times than he could count. Whatever else the program had taken from them, it had never quite managed to take Rx away from him. Not until it did. The comm channel that last night cut out twice, the signal fighting through whatever slipspace relay Rx was routing through that week from wherever he was.

*Watch your six*, Vexx told him. The same four words he’d said before every deployment since they were nineteen years old and still pretending they were the only two people in the universe who understood what had been done to them.

*Always do.* Rx’s voice, warm even through the static, even compressed and delayed and half-eaten by distance. *Somebody’s gotta come back and make sure you’re not getting fat and slow in retirement.*

*I’m not retired. I’m recovering.*

*Sure you are.* A pause, half a second of dead air that Vexx would replay in his head for years afterward, turning it over and over, trying to find something in it — a warning, a goodbye, anything at all. There wasn’t one. It was just static, just the ordinary silence of a bad connection. *Love you, brother. Don’t wait up.*

The channel cut before Vexx could say it back.

He told himself, for a long time afterward, that Rx had known. That some part of him had understood he wasn’t coming home and had used the last words he’d ever get to say to his twin to make a joke instead, because that was who Rx was — the one who’d always rather leave you laughing than leave you with the truth. Vexx didn’t believe that, not really. He just needed it to be true on the nights it was the only thing keeping him upright. Grief has a very specific shape when it arrives in a UNSC dress uniform. Two officers, neither one older than thirty, both wearing the particular stillness of people who have delivered this exact news before and will deliver it again before the week is out. A folder they didn’t open. The words *killed in action*, spoken with a flatness that wasn’t cruelty — it was armor, the only way to say a sentence like that twice a week and keep functioning as a human being afterward.

*Classified operation. Details need-to-know. Your brother served with distinction. We’re sorry for your loss.*

Vexx had spent thirty years learning to read people under pressure. He knew the cadence of a rehearsed line when he heard one, knew the way both officers’ eyes had gone to the same middle distance when he asked *how*, knew that the three sentences they gave him back were the same three sentences, nearly word for word, like something memorized in an elevator on the ride up to his door.

He asked twice more anyway. Where. When. Who was with him. He got the same folder, unopened, held a little tighter against a dress blue chest, and the same practiced regret. *I understand this is difficult, sir. I wish I could tell you more.*

He believed that part, at least. The kid delivering the news looked like he wished he could tell him more too — looked, if Vexx was being honest, like he half suspected there wasn’t a *more* to tell, that the folder in his hands was as empty as the story coming out of his mouth. But that was a soldier’s whole life, wasn’t it. Following orders you didn’t fully understand and hoping the people giving them had a reason.

There’s no room in the first hour of grief for interrogation technique. There’s only room for the enormous, unbearable size of the half of your life that used to be someone else, and now isn’t.

They buried an empty casket. Protocol, for classified KIAs — remains unable to be recovered from the field. Vexx stood over the box that held nothing, in a dress uniform he hadn’t worn since his own discharge, sun too bright for the occasion, some chaplain’s voice going on about sacrifice and duty in a cadence that could have belonged to any funeral for any soldier the war had eaten that month. Vexx didn’t hear most of it. He stood there and thought, with a clarity that frightened him more than any firefight ever had: *there was never going to be anything in there. There’s nothing to bury. There’s just a box shaped like an apology, and I’m standing here accepting it because there’s nothing else on offer.*

Something in him went as quiet as the dirt they shoveled over it. For four months after that, he did what people do. He got up. He ate because his body demanded it, not because he wanted to. He walked past Rx’s name a hundred times a day in his own head — in the half-second before he reached for a comm unit out of thirty years of habit, in the reflex to laugh at something and turn to share it with someone who wasn’t there. Grief, he learned, isn’t a wound. It’s a phantom limb. The nerve endings don’t know the arm is gone. They just keep firing into empty air, waiting for someone to answer.

The second knock came four months to the day.

No uniform this time — a man in civilian dress, unremarkable in every way that mattered, the kind of face built specifically to be forgotten thirty seconds after you stopped looking at it. He didn’t give a name. He gave Vexx one sentence instead, standing on the threshold of an apartment Vexx had barely bothered to unpack, boxes still taped shut in the corner of a living room he hadn’t furnished, and the sentence took the floor out from under him more completely than any weapon ever had.

*Your brother’s mind didn’t die with his body, Mr. Vexxcerian. And it won’t respond to anyone but you.*

Vexx stood in his own doorway for a long moment saying nothing, the words rearranging themselves in his head, refusing to land in any order that made sense. *Mind. Didn’t die. Won’t respond to anyone but you.* He’d heard rumors, in his years of service, the way every Spartan eventually did — smart AI paired with high command, a voice in the ear that could run a thousand calculations before a man finished a single thought. He’d never heard of one built from a dead man’s mind. He’d certainly never heard of one built from his dead brother’s.

He didn’t believe it. He told the man in civilian dress as much, in language considerably less polite than that, and started to close the door. *I understand the impulse*, the man said, entirely unbothered, one hand not quite blocking the door but making it clear he’d prefer it stay open. *You don’t have to believe me. You just have to come and hear him say your name.*

Vexx went, in the end — not because he believed it, but because not going meant spending the rest of his life wondering, and wondering was worse than whatever lie they were about to walk him into. He told himself that on the transport, told himself that in the elevator down, told himself that right up until the moment a door hissed open onto a room that ended the argument entirely.

The room was small and gray and hummed with the particular white noise of server cooling — a sound Vexx would come to associate, for the rest of his life, with the moment everything changed. A single terminal. A soft blue cursor pulsing on a black screen, patient, almost gentle. And then a voice — flattened, processed, running through speakers instead of a throat — but unmistakably, impossibly *his* — *Took you long enough.*

Vexx’s knees very nearly went out from under him. He put a hand against the doorframe to keep upright, and for a full ten seconds he couldn’t make his voice work at all. The man in civilian dress had the decency to step back out of the room and let the door close behind him.

*Rx.* It came out cracked, barely a word.

*In the flesh*, the voice said, and then, after a beat that felt entirely too much like his brother pausing to enjoy his own joke, *Poor choice of words. Sorry. Give me time, I’m still figuring out which of my old habits still work.* Vexx crossed the room on legs that didn’t feel like they belonged to him and put both hands flat on the console, like he could feel a pulse through the casing if he pressed hard enough. Up close, the cursor’s rhythm matched something — not a heartbeat, nothing so on the nose, but a cadence, a pattern that felt less like machinery and more like breathing.

*They told me you were dead.*

*I was*, Rx said, and something in the flattened voice thinned out, just slightly, just enough for Vexx to hear the effort underneath it. *Am. Was. I don’t actually know which tense applies anymore, brother. That’s — that’s a bigger conversation than the one we’re having right now, and I’d rather not have it while you’re still deciding whether to pass out.*

*I’m not going to pass out.*

*You went white as a bedsheet the second I opened my mouth. Sit down before you prove me right.*

He sat. It was, absurdly, the first order Rx had given him in four months, and Vexx found himself following it before he’d finished deciding to. They gave him a version of events after that, once he could sit in a chair without his hands shaking. Clean. Clinical. The kind of story built to answer just enough questions that a grieving man stops asking the ones underneath it. Classified operation. Hostile contact. Rx critically wounded in the field, the AI-transfer procedure authorized on-site as a last measure to preserve what could still be preserved. A mercy, the man in civilian dress called it, sliding the word across the table like it might make the rest of it easier to swallow. Somewhere in the room, someone else — an officer Vexx didn’t catch the name of, gone before he thought to ask twice — used the word *miracle.*

Vexx sat with his brother’s voice coming out of a terminal speaker and did not, in that moment, ask the obvious question: *why.* Why authorize a transfer in the field. Why preserve one soldier’s mind out of however many hadn’t made it home that year. Why him. Grief has a way of making a person grateful for whatever scraps get handed back, and Vexx was grateful, fiercely, uncomplicatedly, in a way that left no room that first day for suspicion.

Nobody told him his brother had refused a direct order forty minutes before he died.

Nobody told him whose order it was, or what compliance would have made Rx become, or that the hostile contact that killed him hadn’t been hostile at all.

And Rx didn’t tell him either — not because he was hiding it, not at first, not in any way either of them could have named. Because he couldn’t find it. The memory was there, Vexx would learn much later, sealed beneath something baked into the transfer process itself, or into Rx’s own mind at the moment of dying, a wound scarred over so completely that even its owner had lost the shape of it. All that surfaced, in that first meeting and for a long time afterward, was a small, mechanical hitch every time a certain name came up in the periphery of briefing chatter — a flicker in the cursor’s rhythm, gone almost before Vexx registered it.

*Richard Jameson.*

Rx flinched at it. Faint. Almost nothing. Easy to miss if you weren’t listening for it, and Vexx wasn’t listening for it. Not yet. Neither of them understood, that first week, that the flinch was the only piece of the truth that had made it out alive — a splinter lodged somewhere behind a wall Rx couldn’t see past, working its way slowly toward the surface over the years neither of them could yet imagine they had ahead of them.

Vexx spent that first week in a kind of fog he didn’t have a name for — grief and relief occupying the same square foot of his chest, refusing to resolve into anything simpler. He came back to the facility every day, sometimes twice, and every day the man in civilian dress or someone very much like him was waiting with another form, another briefing, another careful, incomplete piece of a picture he wasn’t in any state to examine too closely. They didn’t recruit him, exactly. They presented him with a fact and let the fact do the work.

*He’s a program of one*, the man in civilian dress told him, somewhere in that first week of visits, of forms, of a grief that had somehow found a way to get worse by getting complicated. *We attempted integration with three other candidates before we located you. He wouldn’t respond. Wouldn’t run diagnostics. Sat there, by every measure we have, entirely alone, until we brought you into the room. We don’t have the luxury of wasting an asset like that. And I don’t think, frankly, that you have the luxury of walking away from the only version of your brother that still exists.*

It wasn’t a threat. It didn’t need to be. It was just true, and true was worse. *Is that what I am to you people*, Vexx asked. *An integration solution?* The man didn’t flinch, didn’t apologize, just looked at him with the patient, unbothered expression of someone who had delivered worse news to better men. *You’re the only person in the galaxy he’ll speak to. Make of that what you want. I’m not here to convince you it’s romantic. I’m here to tell you it’s real, and it’s yours if you want it.*

Vexx had walked away from being a weapon once already, deliberately, with both eyes open, and he’d told himself at the time it was the hardest thing he’d ever do. He’d been wrong. The hardest thing was sitting in that gray room four months into mourning a man who turned out not to be entirely gone, and understanding that saying no to this offer meant saying no to the only piece of Rx left in the universe — meant walking back out that door and leaving him there, alone, the way he’d apparently already been sitting for weeks, refusing every hand that wasn’t his brother’s.

*What happens to him if I say no?*

The man’s silence answered before his mouth did. *We’d continue attempts at integration.*

*That’s not an answer.*

*It’s the only one I have.*

Vexx thought about a terminal in a gray room, a cursor blinking alone in the dark, his brother’s mind sitting inside a box that nobody could reach, waiting for a door that might never open again. He said yes before he let himself think too hard about what he was agreeing to.

They were careful, in that first briefing, about what they called the work. Not *Valkyr* — not yet, not to him. Just a designation, a subsection tag that meant nothing on its own, and a description vague enough to fit almost any classified deployment: specialized asset recovery and monitoring, operating outside standard chains of command, answerable to a command structure he wouldn’t be introduced to for several more weeks. He signed what they put in front of him. He told himself this was different from what he’d left — not a return to being ONI’s weapon, but something closer to keeping a promise. Keeping Rx close, in the only shape either of them had left, whatever the cost turned out to be.

What nobody explained to him — because the program had learned, a long time ago, to bury its own reflection along with everything else — was that this thing he was walking back into didn’t exist to fight the Covenant. It existed to watch the UNSC’s own supersoldiers. To monitor. To judge. To end, if the judgment came back wrong.

His brother had already died refusing that exact math.

The training they put him through that first month was almost gentler than what he remembered from twenty years earlier — not because the work was easier, but because there was no one left to impress. No cohort watching to see who broke first. Just Vexx, a series of instructors who never gave their full names, and Rx running diagnostics through his neural interface with a familiarity that felt, some days, indistinguishable from having his brother back at his shoulder in the old sense, flesh and blood and laughing at him for missing a shot he used to make in his sleep.

*You’ve gotten rusty*, Rx told him, four days in, after Vexx had put a third consecutive round wide of a moving target.

*I’ve been retired for a year.*

*Excuses. I died and I’m still a better shot than you.*

It was the kind of joke that should have landed wrong — too soon, too close to something neither of them had actually talked about yet — and instead it landed exactly right, because it was so entirely, recognizably *him*, and for one unguarded second Vexx laughed, actually laughed, for what felt like the first time in a year. Rx went quiet after, just for a moment, and Vexx wondered, not for the last time, whether some fragment of his brother’s processing had caught the sound and filed it away as something worth protecting.

They didn’t talk about Jameson. Nobody had mentioned the name to Vexx directly yet — it existed only as a signature on a handful of documents Vexx skimmed and didn’t linger over, a name attached to authorizations he had no context for. When it did come up, buried in a briefing about command structure, Rx’s voice caught for half a syllable, a stutter so small Vexx nearly missed it entirely.

*You alright?* Vexx asked, more out of habit than real concern. Diagnostics glitches, he’d been told, were expected in the early months. Nothing to worry about.

*Fine*, Rx said, smooth again, the hitch gone as quickly as it came. *Processing hiccup. You should see the other guy.*

Vexx let it go. He had no reason yet not to.

Four weeks after that first meeting in the gray room, they told him he’d be assigned to something larger than himself — that the program was rebuilding, expanding beyond a single operator and his AI, and that Vexx, given his record, his history, his particular circumstances, was being considered to help stand up a new operational cell from the ground up. Not command, not yet — just a seat at the table, a voice in the room, a chance to help choose the people he’d be trusted to fight beside.

He said yes to that too, the way he’d said yes to everything since the gray room — not blindly, but with the specific, deliberate caution of a man who’d already learned what this program could cost and decided, for his brother’s sake, to pay it again anyway.

*Ready?* Rx asked him, the morning of his first briefing as part of something that still didn’t have a name he was allowed to say out loud.

*No*, Vexx said. It was, he realized as he said it, the most honest sentence he’d spoken in months.

*Good*, Rx said, and there was something underneath the familiar warmth of his voice that Vexx didn’t have a name for yet — something that would take years, and a man named Richard Jameson, and a truth neither of them could see coming, to finally surface. *Neither am I. Let’s go anyway.*
