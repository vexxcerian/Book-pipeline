# Chapter Five

Gaia’s friction with him came to a head on a mission that, on paper, should never have gone sideways at all.

It was a simple monitoring assignment — track a SPARTAN-II fireteam through a routine engagement, assess for stability, report back. No target flagged for elevated risk. No Choosing authorization attached to the brief. The kind of mission Vexx had started, privately, to think of as the good kind — the kind where the cell’s presence never had to become anything more than quiet, invisible observation.

Gaia went in ahead of the rest of them, as she always did, threading through a ruined industrial complex on the edge of a contested zone to get eyes on the fireteam before contact. She found something she wasn’t supposed to find — a second element, unmarked, moving parallel to the fireteam’s position with a patience that read, to her trained eye, as anything but friendly.

“I’ve got unknowns,” she said over comm, voice flat and fast. “Not our target. Second element, six-plus, moving to flank the fireteam we’re supposed to be watching. I can take the high ground and get eyes on their objective, or I can fall back to rally with the rest of you. Your call, but every second I spend waiting on it is a second they close the gap.”

“Fall back,” Vexx said. “We regroup, assess together, then move as a unit.” “That’s going to cost us the intel.”

“That’s the order.”

There was a pause on the channel just long enough to carry weight. “Copy,” Gaia said, and fell back — but Vexx heard, even through the clipped professionalism of it, the disagreement sitting underneath the word. By the time the cell regrouped and moved as a unit, the unknown element had shifted position, folding back into terrain Gaia could no longer read cleanly, and whatever intelligence might have been gathered in that first window was gone. The fireteam they’d been sent to monitor made it through their engagement unharmed, which meant the mission, by every metric command cared about, counted as a success. It didn’t feel like one riding home in the transport, Gaia sitting apart from the rest of the cell with her arms crossed and her jaw set in a way Vexx was already learning to read as controlled fury rather than the absence of it.

She found him afterward, in the corridor outside the debrief room, and didn’t bother easing into it.

“You cost us that intel because you didn’t trust me to work alone for ninety seconds.”

“I made the call I thought kept the cell safest.”

“I’ve spent my whole career working alone. That’s not a weakness you need to manage, it’s the entire reason command put me on this roster.” Her voice stayed level, but there was real heat under it, the kind that came from somewhere older than one mission. “You said, back when you recruited me, that you weren’t going to pretend you could talk me out of my instincts. You’re doing exactly that. Just slower, and with better manners.” Vexx didn’t answer right away. He thought about it — actually thought about it, rather than reaching for the easy defense of rank — because some part of him recognized that she wasn’t entirely wrong, even if she wasn’t entirely right either.

“I’m not trying to manage your instincts,” he said finally. “I made a call because I don’t yet know what’s out there in these engagements that isn’t showing up on any brief we’re given. I’d rather lose intel than lose one of you to something none of us understand yet.”

“That’s not your call to make for me.”

“It’s exactly my call to make. That’s what command means.” He kept his voice even, but something in it must have carried more weight than he intended, because Gaia’s arms uncrossed, slightly, some of the heat in her expression cooling into something closer to actual attention. “I’m not going to apologize for erring toward caution. I will apologize if it becomes a pattern that stops you from doing the job you’re good at. Today, I don’t think it was that. Today, I think something’s moving out there that none of us have a name for yet, and I’d rather be too careful than find out the hard way what it is.”

She studied him for a long moment, some calculation running behind her eyes that Vexx couldn’t fully read. “You know something,” she said finally. Not a question.

“I suspect something,” Vexx said. “There’s a difference, and I don’t have enough on the suspect side yet to bring it to the rest of the cell. When I do, you’ll be the first to hear it — not because I owe you an apology, but because I think you’ll be the one who reads it fastest and best.” That, more than anything else he could have said, seemed to land. Gaia gave him a short, unimpressed nod — not agreement, exactly, more like a temporary suspension of the argument — and walked off toward the barracks without another word. Echo, quiet through the whole exchange, offered a small, approving hum in Vexx’s ear that mirrored, almost exactly, the sound Rx had made weeks earlier after Vexx’s first honest conversation with her.

*She’s not wrong to push*, Rx said, once the corridor had emptied.

*I know.*

*Doesn’t mean you were wrong to hold the line, either.* A pause. *You’re getting better at this. The command part. I wasn’t sure you would. High praise from a man who won’t tell me what he’s actually hiding.* The silence that followed wasn’t angry. It was just long, and tired, and Vexx let it sit rather than push, the way he’d learned to do more and more often that autumn.

He found Aglaope two nights later, in the small common room the cell had adopted as its unofficial gathering space, sitting alone with a cup of something long gone cold, staring at nothing in particular with the same stillness Vexx had first noticed in her file, months earlier, before he’d understood what it meant.

“You’re not sleeping,” she said, without looking up, as he came in. “Neither are you.”

“I don’t need as much of it as you do.” A small, tired smile. “Sit. You look like you’re carrying something you haven’t set down in a while.”

He sat. He hadn’t intended to say anything — had told himself, walking in, that he just wanted company that didn’t ask questions — but something about the quality of her attention, the same unhurried, unshakeable steadiness he’d watched her offer to Corwin in a jungle clearing and to Reyes in the dirt of a ruined settlement, made the words come up out of him before he’d fully decided to let them.

“I think someone in this program killed my brother,” he said, quiet, the words strange and heavy in his own mouth, the first time he’d said any version of it out loud to another living person. “Not in combat. Not the way I was told. I think someone gave an order, and he refused it, and someone made sure that refusal never got the chance to matter.”

Aglaope didn’t flinch, didn’t reach for reassurance or disbelief, just let the words sit in the room the way she let everything sit — giving them room to be as heavy as they actually were instead of rushing to make them lighter. “What makes you think that?”

“Fragments. A dog tag that isn’t in any record. A dead man’s voice giving an order that sounds like it came from someone who’d done it before. My own brother’s memory sealed shut around something that flinches every time a certain name comes up in a room.” He shook his head slowly. “None of it’s proof. All of it points the same direction.”

“Toward Jameson.”

He looked at her, surprised she’d said it outright, and she met his eyes without apology. “You don’t have to be a mind reader to see it, Vexx. You go quiet in a very specific way whenever his name comes up. I noticed weeks ago. I didn’t ask, because I figured you’d tell me when you were ready, and pushing you before that would only make you close up tighter.”

“Why didn’t it worry you? That I might be — I don’t know. Grieving my way into seeing something that isn’t there.”

“Because grief makes people see things that aren’t there,” Aglaope said, quiet, certain. “It doesn’t usually make them this careful about proving themselves wrong first. You’ve spent months building a case you haven’t shown anyone, instead of running straight at the first name that felt right. That’s not grief talking. That’s a man who wants to be certain before he lets himself be angry.”

Vexx sat with that for a long moment, something in his chest loosening slightly for the first time in weeks — not resolution, nothing so clean, just the particular relief of finally being witnessed by someone who didn’t need him to have an answer yet, only needed him to have said the thing out loud. “You can’t tell the others,” he said. “Not yet. Not until I understand more.” “I wasn’t going to,” Aglaope said. “That’s not what tonight was for.” She reached over, briefly, and rested a hand on his arm — the same simple, steady gesture she’d offered Corwin, offered Reyes, offered, Vexx understood suddenly, everyone she’d ever sat beside so they didn’t have to carry something alone. “Requiem always says she wants to keep singing for people, not just against them. I think that’s what tonight was too. You don’t have to carry this by yourself just because you can’t say it out loud to everyone yet.”

He didn’t tell her everything — didn’t mention the dog tag by name, didn’t repeat the fragments of Rx’s fractured, unplaceable memory in full. But he told her enough, and it was, he realized walking back to his own quarters much later that night, the first time since the empty casket that the weight of it had felt like something other than entirely his to carry alone. *You told her*, Rx said, once he was alone.

*I needed to tell someone. Before it started eating me from the inside the way it’s clearly eating you.*

Another long silence — the kind Vexx had learned, over the past weeks, to recognize as his brother choosing his next words with unusual care. *I’m glad it was her*, Rx said finally. *Out of everyone on this roster. I think she’ll keep it safe.*

*You could tell me what you’re carrying too, you know. Whenever you’re ready.*

*I know.* Quiet, almost too quiet to catch. *I’m working on being ready, Vexx. I promise you that.*

Vexx lay awake a long time after that, turning the promise over in his mind the way he’d turned over every fragment of evidence in the drawer beside his bed — a dog tag, a data chip, a half-formed suspicion with a name attached to it that he still wasn’t ready to say out loud in any room where it might be overheard. Somewhere above him, in an office with better lighting and a warmer smile than the truth deserved, Richard Jameson slept, as he had for four years, entirely certain that whatever he’d buried in a jungle clearing a lifetime ago had stayed buried.

He was wrong. He simply didn’t know it yet.
