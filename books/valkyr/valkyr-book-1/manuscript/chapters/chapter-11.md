“—it isn’t a door now, it’s a hole,” Gaia said. “A door does something. That’s a hole with a hinge on it.”

“It’s propped.”

“I know it’s propped.” She had her back against the wall, her arms crossed, her weight on the foot nearest the stair, which was where she stood in every room he had ever been in with her. “Stand there. No. There. Now listen to it.”

He stood where she put him — listened, with his head still, the way she meant it — and what came back was a corridor in an operations block at the wrong end of a working day: a transport turning over out on the pad, the generator deck running under everything as it was always running under everything, water going somewhere two floors down, the flat unpitched noise of people eating a long way off through the open door.

“What am I listening to?”

“All of it. That’s the complaint.” She did not move off the wall. “Shut, this corridor is dry. You get the stair landing, you get whoever’s on the stair, you get nothing else, and the nothing else is the point of it. Open, you’ve got the pad in here, the deck, the water, a mess hall full of people who think they’re being quiet. I can’t hear the stair through that.”

“And you need to hear the stair.”

“I don’t need to hear the stair. I hear the stair.” She said it flatly, without any interest in whether it landed. “I’ve been hearing that stair since we moved in. The closer on it is slack, so it lands twice, and Goliath drags his heel on the sill, so I know it’s him coming. That’s a room I know. This isn’t a room now. It sounds like weather.”

*Is this the Gullet?* Echo said, arriving on the net mid-thought, as he arrived everywhere. *Because if this is about the Gullet, I want it minuted that I have had opinions about the Gullet since the morning we walked in, that I have never been asked for them, that I have sat on them like eggs—*

“Echo. Off.”

*Off,* Echo said, and was.

Nobody had ever agreed to call the corridor the Gullet.

“Shut it, then,” Vexx said. “It’s there. Shut it and we’re done.”

“I’ve shut it. I shut it the night before last, and it was open again before breakfast, and I shut it yesterday, and there it is.”

“Then shut it again.”

“And tomorrow.”

“Somebody wants it open.”

“Whoever it is wants it open enough to put a boot in it,” Gaia said, “and not enough to come down here and say so.”

They both looked at the boot: a left boot, standard issue, honestly worn, its laces gone, cord through the eyelets instead, the sole gone smooth at the ball like a boot that has done a great deal of standing — wedged up into the gap with the whole weight of a heavy self-closer sitting down on it.

“Whose is it?”

“Nobody’s.”

“Somebody’s.”

“I’ve asked around. It hasn’t got an owner.”

Goliath came down the stair with a cup in one hand. He saw the two of them, saw where they were standing, slowed the way you slow going past an argument you have decided in advance to have no part of.

“Goliath.”

“No,” Goliath said.

“You don’t know what I was going to ask you.”

“I know what everybody in this corridor has been asked for a week.” He drank some of what was in the cup and pulled a face at it that had nothing to do with either of them, or with the door, and was entirely about the cup. “Not mine.”

“I’m not asking you to own it,” Gaia said. “We’re asking whether you’ve seen a man put it there.”

“I’ve seen the door open. I’ve seen the boot in it.” He thought about it properly, standing there, because he did not answer questions any other way. “Whoever’s doing it isn’t doing it for air. You want air, you use a chair, or you wedge the top of it and it goes unnoticed for a month. A boot at the bottom is what you use when you want to come through it fast and not stop.”

Neither of them had anything to say to that, and Goliath did not appear to have expected any.

“Coffee’s an insult,” Goliath said, to nobody, and went on down toward the mess, and the door let the noise of it in after him.

“Thanks,” Gaia said, to his back.

“It’s a fire door,” Vexx said. “It shouldn’t be propped at all. I’ll have it shut.”

“Why?”

“Because it’s a fire door.”

“The fire’s got nothing to do with it.”

“It’s the reason I can put something out about it.”

“So you’ll put something out about the fire.” She took her shoulder off the wall, finally, one step, which from her was a shout. “Then it’s shut for the fire, and the fire isn’t standing in this corridor tomorrow morning, and I am, and the boot comes back.”

“The boot comes back either way.”

“The boot comes back because the only thing anybody in this building has ever heard about that door is a regulation.”

“I’ll have it shut, Gaia. You’ve got what you came for.”

“I’ve got the door shut.”

“That’s what you asked for.”

“It’s what I said.” She stopped there. Then, in a different voice altogether, reasonable, which with her meant she had decided to try being reasonable at him: “Say it’s a bad door.”

“I’m not—”

“One sentence. *It’s a bad door.* You can have thought of it yourself, I don’t care, say it and I’ll go.”

“I’m not going to stand in a corridor and—”

“You’re already standing in a corridor.”

“It’s shut in an hour. What does the sentence get you.”

“Nothing. That’s why it costs you nothing. That’s why you could have said it four minutes ago.”

“Then let it be an order and take the win.”

“I don’t want the door shut because you’ve ordered it,” Gaia said. “I want it shut because it’s a bad door.”

“It’s the same door either way.”

She looked back at him without any hurry at all, with the whole evening in front of her, nowhere she would rather spend it.

“Whose boot is it, though,” she said. “Genuinely. Somebody in this building is walking around in one boot.”

“Yes.”

“For days.”

“Yes.”

“Doesn’t that—”

“It does,” Vexx said, “yes,” and went and took hold of the boot. He had to put a shoulder into the door to get slack enough to work it free, took longer at it than he should have, did not look good doing it — then the self-closer took the door the rest of the way, a long hydraulic sigh, a soft final knock of metal into frame.

The corridor changed, and he heard it change — the difference between a room with something in it, a room with nothing in it — and he had walked this one up and down for weeks without hearing it at all. Somewhere above them the stair door landed, twice, small, exact, like a coin dropped on a table two rooms away.

Gaia had her head over slightly, the way she did on a ridgeline.

“Dead,” she said, with an enormous satisfaction she made no attempt to cover. “Listen to that. That’s dead.”

“That’s good.”

“That’s very good.”

“I’m standing here holding a boot,” Vexx said.

That was what did it: it came out of her first — a short unlovely bark of a laugh she was plainly not expecting, that she made no attempt at all to stop — and then it got him, and the two of them stood in a dead corridor at the end of a working day laughing at a boot, until it ran out.

It ran out. He was still holding the boot, and he kept on holding it.

“At the relay hall,” Gaia said, “I stood at that corner with a full view of a wall.”

“I know.”

“You put me there so you could see me.”

He did not answer that, because there was no version of the answer that was not yes.

“I had things ready to say to you on the ramp. I said none of them, I’ve been carrying them around ever since, which is how I know it wasn’t about the wall.” She stood square to him now. “Here’s what I think. You’ve had something since before the jungle. You decided not to bring it to us — you didn’t decide it once, you’ve decided it every week since, which is the harder way to do it, and you do it anyway. It isn’t that you think we’d sell you. It’s that you want us able to stand in front of a woman with a notebook, say we didn’t know, be telling the truth when we say it. You worked out what each of us would cost you. In your head, in order. I’d put money on where I came.”

“You pulled our files again after the jungle,” she said. “All of us. Nobody asked you to, nobody told you to, you did it on your own time — and the only reason to do that is to find out which of us goes to command first.”

It was close — close enough that the part she had wrong sat in his mouth with nowhere to put it down — she had the machine of it exactly right, the reason for it wrong, the reason for it not on her roster, not on anybody’s roster these four years — and he kept it where it was, and let her keep the rest.

“You told me I’d be first,” she said.

“I did.”

“So.”

The dispenser on the level below had been out of its middle selection for a year, with a strip of paper taped down over the button — ordered, presumably, signed for, presumably, a part sitting in a cage somewhere under a number nobody had matched up yet.

“One of the jobs we’ve run,” Vexx said, “wasn’t the job we were briefed.”

The sentence came out whole, and he had not built it standing there — he had been carrying it for weeks, cut down and cut down again, the size of a thing you could hand across a corridor. Until it was out of him he had not understood what he had been trimming it for.

“Which one.”

“No.”

“Who briefed it.”

“No.”

“Have you got paper.”

“Nothing I can give you.”

“That’s a no.”

“That’s a no.”

She took it. She stood there turning it over in front of him without hurrying — he watched her find the edges of it, find how small it was, not say so.

“That was your one,” Gaia said.

He waited.

“You had one. That was it. I’m not coming back for the rest — not in a corridor, not on a net, not after something goes wrong and we’re all standing around a transport wondering. If it turns out to be what I think it is, I’ll find out when everybody else finds out, and I’ll be wherever you’ve put me.” She said it without heat, which was worse. “What you do with the rest of it is your call.”

She was nearly at the stair when she stopped.

“When I was nineteen I was in transient housing where the walls were nothing,” she said. “There was a man through mine who laughed once a night, about eleven. One laugh. Never two. I was in there five months.”

Vexx waited for the rest of it.

There was no rest of it. She went up the stair, and the slack closer landed twice behind her — dry, clean, in a corridor with nothing in it.

“It’s a bad door,” he said.

The trauma pack in the bottom of his locker was sealed, dated, with a tab across the lid that you break for one reason. He broke it, lifted the wadding, put the tag in underneath. Then the scorched fragment of plating, the half-melted chip, the pressure dressing laid back down over them. Then the walk to the supply cage for a fresh tab off the roll, the walk back, the tab struck on square. None of that took any thinking at all, and he had chosen the pack before he had crossed the room.

*You’ve gone quiet,* he said.

Nothing came back. The cursor on the pad by the bed went on doing what it did.

If it was ever asked about, the answer was that he had used the pack on the Kettle job, drawn a new one at the cage — and he said it once, out loud, in the room, to hear what the room did with it.

The room did nothing with it. It went a pace, stopped, dry, no carry, a dead little room with a bed in it and a locker in it — and the sentence sounded like the truth.
