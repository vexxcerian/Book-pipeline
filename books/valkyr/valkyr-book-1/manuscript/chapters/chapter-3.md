# Chapter Three

The mission brief, stripped of Jameson’s opening remarks, came down to something almost disappointingly simple: a SPARTAN-II operator, designation withheld even from Vexx’s cell, had gone dark on a routine deployment eleven days earlier. No distress signal. No wreckage. Just silence where a mandatory check-in should have been, and a growing unease in whatever office decided such things mattered enough to send six strangers and their AI out to find an answer.

“Your job isn’t combat,” Jameson told them, standing at the head of the briefing table with a hard-light projection of a jungle world turning slowly above it. “Your job is answers. Find him. Assess him. Report back before anyone else gets involved.” A pause, brief, exact. “If assessment indicates a threat to himself, his unit, or the mission at large, you are authorized — not obligated, authorized — to make the call the situation requires. I trust every one of you to know the difference.”

Nobody in the room asked what *the call the situation requires* meant in plain language. They didn’t need to. It was the entire reason any of them had been recruited in the first place, dressed up in the kind of bureaucratic softness that let everyone in the room pretend, for one more day, that they might never actually have to use the words *Choosing* out loud.

Vexx caught Paladin’s jaw tighten, almost imperceptibly, at the phrase. He filed it away. Not yet a problem.

*You alright?* he asked Rx, privately, as the briefing broke and the cell began filing out toward the transport bay.

*Fine*, Rx said. Too quick. *Let’s get moving.*

* * *

The dropship carried them low over a canopy so dense it looked, from altitude, like a single unbroken green skin stretched across the whole world. Gaia had her forehead against the viewport, watching the terrain scroll past with the flat, assessing stare of someone already three moves ahead of the landing.

“Recovery unit went in eleven days ago,” she said, to no one in particular. “No wreckage. No signal. Man doesn’t just vanish in triple-canopy jungle without leaving something behind. Unless he didn’t want to be found.”

“Or something didn’t want him found,” Zeus said, from across the bay, not looking up from the data pad he’d been quietly annotating since wheels-up. “Two very different mission profiles. Be a shame to walk in assuming the wrong one.”

“That’s what we’re here to find out,” Vexx said, and the bay went quiet in the particular way a team goes quiet right before a drop — six operators checking gear they’d already checked twice, six AI running silent diagnostics through six sets of neural interfaces, everyone privately making peace, in their own way, with whatever the next several hours were about to cost them.

Goliath caught Vexx’s eye across the bay and gave him a short nod — not reassurance, exactly, just acknowledgment, one operator to another, that whatever came off that ramp, they’d be facing it together. Vexx nodded back. It was, he realized, the first time in longer than he could easily remember that going into an unknown situation hadn’t felt entirely like something he had to carry alone.

*First deployment as a full cell*, Rx said, quiet, just for him. *Feels different, doesn’t it. Having six of them at your back instead of just me.*

*Feels good*, Vexx admitted. *Doesn’t mean I trust it yet.*

*Smart man.*

* * *

They found the recovery unit’s last known position by midafternoon — a burned-out clearing where a temporary camp had clearly stood, scorched in a pattern too deliberate to be accidental, equipment stripped or destroyed with a thoroughness that read less like combat and more like someone erasing evidence of their own presence.

Gaia crouched at the tree line, reading the ground the way other people read a page. “One set of prints leaving the clearing. Heavy gait — armored, moving fast, not limping, not favoring an injury. Whoever burned this camp walked away from it under their own power.”

“Our target?” Paladin asked.

“Only one it could be.” She straightened, scanning the canopy overhead with the particular stillness Vexx was already learning meant she’d caught something the rest of them hadn’t. “He’s still out there. And he wants people to know he did this on purpose.”

Goliath moved into the clearing behind her, crouching over a half-melted crate that had once held field rations, running a gloved hand along a scorch pattern radiating out from its center. “This wasn’t a standard incendiary,” he said. “Whoever set this knew exactly how much charge to use to destroy the contents without torching the whole tree line. That’s not panic. That’s someone who does this kind of thing for a living.”

“Spector confirm?” Vexx asked.

“Confirmed,” Spector said, through Goliath’s interface, clipped and certain. “Charge pattern’s textbook. If I didn’t know better, I’d say a demolitions specialist laid this.”

“He was Spartan-trained,” Zeus said, crouched a short distance away, turning over a fragment of communications equipment in gloved fingers, methodical, unhurried. “Every discipline gets cross-trained in field demolition at some level. Doesn’t take a specialist. Just takes someone who paid attention in the right class.” He held the fragment up to the light, turning it. “This is more interesting. Comms array’s been deliberately smashed, not burned. Somebody wanted to make sure no signal ever went out of this clearing, even after the fire went cold.”

“Which tells us what?” Paladin asked.

“Tells us he wasn’t just hiding from whoever attacked him,” Zeus said. “He was making sure nobody could triangulate this position after the fact. That’s not the behavior of a man running from an ambush. That’s the behavior of a man who thinks *everyone* might be looking for him — the enemy and his own side both.”

Vexx felt something in his chest tighten at that, an old, familiar unease he didn’t yet have a reason to name. He said nothing, only nodded for Gaia to pick the trail back up.

They tracked the trail for six hours through jungle that fought them for every meter — vines that had to be cut, ground that gave way without warning into sinkholes hidden under decades of leaf litter, the constant low static of insect life that made even Echo’s usually cheerful narration go clipped and businesslike. Vexx felt the old rhythms of fieldwork settle back into his body the way they always had, muscle memory outlasting a year of retirement without effort, the particular quiet focus of moving through hostile terrain with people he was starting, despite everything, to trust.

They found him a little after dusk.

He was sitting with his back against a fallen tree, armor cracked in a dozen places, helmet off beside him, staring at nothing in particular with the flat, thousand-meter stare of a man who had stopped, at some point in the last eleven days, being entirely sure of where he was. His AI’s chassis sat dark and unresponsive on his knee — powered down, or damaged, or something worse that none of them had a name for yet.

“Easy,” Vexx said, stepping into the clearing with both hands visible, weapon lowered but not holstered. “We’re not here to hurt you.”

The Spartan’s eyes tracked to him slowly, focus swimming back from somewhere very far away. “Valkyr,” he said. It wasn’t a question. “Took you long enough.”

The name landed in the clearing like a dropped weight. Vexx heard Zeus go very still behind him, heard Gaia’s breath catch half a second before she covered it. None of them had used that word in front of him. None of them had used that word *anywhere* he could plausibly have heard it.

“How do you know that name,” Vexx said, carefully, every instinct in him narrowing to a single point of focus.

The Spartan laughed — a short, broken sound with nothing funny in it. “Because I’ve been waiting for someone like you people to finally show up. Eleven days out here, no comms, no support, and you know what I kept thinking? *They’ll send the ghosts eventually. The ones who watch. The ones who decide.*” His eyes found Vexx’s, and there was something in them — not fear, not quite, something closer to exhausted recognition. “So which is it. You here to bring me in, or you here to finish what my own chain of command already tried to start?”

* * *

It took the better part of an hour to get the real story out of him, delivered in fragments between long silences, his voice hoarse from eleven days of talking to no one but a dead AI and his own echoing thoughts. His unit had been sent into the jungle to secure a listening post. They’d walked into an ambush instead — not Covenant, not insurgents, but a strike team wearing UNSC signatures that didn’t match any deployment on record. His squad hadn’t survived it. He had, barely, and spent the following days convinced, with the particular clarity that comes from proximity to death, that whoever had ordered that ambush would come finish the job the moment they realized he had.

“I burned the camp myself,” he said, quiet, staring at his hands. “Didn’t want them tracking anything back to a specific unit if — when — they found what was left of me. Didn’t want anyone else dragged into it.”

Vexx crouched down across from him, weapon fully lowered now, and asked the question that mattered most. “Do you have proof? Anything that ties the ambush to a UNSC signature?”

“Fragments.” A short, humorless laugh. “Enough to know I’m not crazy. Not enough to matter to anyone who could actually do something about it.”

Rx had gone very quiet through the entire exchange — quieter than Vexx had ever heard him, even in the earliest days after the pairing. Vexx noticed it, filed it away as concern rather than alarm, and was midway through formulating his report back to command when Rx’s voice came through, low, strange, threaded with something Vexx had never heard in it before.

*Vexx.*

*What is it.*

*I saw something. Just now. When he said “the ones who watch, the ones who decide” — I* — Rx stopped. Started again. *I saw a treeline. Not this one. Somewhere else, somewhere I don’t have a file for. And a voice. I couldn’t place the voice, but it was giving an order, and I —* Another stop, longer this time, the digital equivalent of someone catching their breath. *It’s gone now. I don’t know what that was.*

*Rx.*

*I’m fine.* Too quick again — the same reflex as the transport bay, the same reflex as Jameson’s office three weeks earlier, but sharper now, more urgent, like something straining against a door that had held for months and had just, for one fractional second, not held quite as well. *I’m fine, Vexx. Focus on the operator. We’ll talk about it after.*

Vexx wanted to push. Every instinct in him wanted to stop everything happening in that clearing and ask his brother what he’d just seen, what a treeline with no file attached to it meant, why his voice had gone thin and strange in a way it never had before. He didn’t. There was a broken man in front of him who needed an answer more urgently than Vexx needed one of his own, and thirty years of training had taught him, if nothing else, how to set aside his own unease until the mission allowed for it.

He filed it away. Not yet a problem. A shape on the horizon, closer now than it had been that morning, though he still didn’t have the vocabulary to name it.

* * *

Zeus took over the questioning once the operator had a canteen of water in him and enough of his voice back to speak in full sentences, and Vexx watched him work with the particular unease of a man witnessing a skill he’d been right to be cautious about and right to bring along anyway.

Zeus didn’t push. That was the surprising part. He sat, unhurried, at a companionable distance rather than looming, and asked questions in an order that seemed almost aimless until Vexx realized, watching the operator’s shoulders slowly uncoil over the better part of twenty minutes, that Zeus was building trust before he built a timeline — letting the man talk about his squad, their names, small human details that had nothing to do with the ambush, before circling, gently, back toward the thing that mattered.

“The strike team,” Zeus said finally, voice even, unthreatening. “You said UNSC signatures. Talk me through what you actually saw, not what you’ve had eleven days alone in the jungle to decide it meant.”

The operator’s jaw worked for a moment. “Armor plating. Standard-issue, but modified — the kind of modification only a handful of black programs get clearance for. And radio chatter, before everything went to hell, using UNSC tactical shorthand. Not perfect. Not textbook. But close enough that my squad didn’t even raise weapons until it was too late, because everyone’s brain said *friendly* right up until the moment it very much wasn’t.”

“Anyone give an order you could make out? A name, a callsign, anything?”

The operator’s eyes went distant again, chasing something at the edge of memory. “One voice. Cutting through the chaos, giving the kill order clean as anything, like it was nothing. I didn’t get a name. Just—” He stopped, shook his head slowly. “Just the *certainty* of it. Like whoever it was had done this before and would do it again without losing a night’s sleep over it.”

Vexx felt Rx go very still in his ear at that — not the earlier flicker, not quite, but a held-breath quality that lasted a beat too long to be nothing. He didn’t ask about it there, in front of the operator, in front of the rest of the cell. He simply filed it, the way he’d been filing everything that autumn, and made himself focus on the man in front of him instead.

It was Aglaope, in the end, who did the part none of the rest of them could. While Zeus finished extracting what usable detail remained, she sat beside the operator and simply stayed — no questions, no urgency, just a steady, unhurried presence that seemed to say, without a single word, *you don’t have to carry this alone anymore.* Vexx watched her hand rest lightly on the man’s shoulder as the adrenaline finally broke and the eleven days caught up with him all at once, watched him put his face in his hands and shake, silently, for a long moment, and watched Aglaope simply stay, the way Requiem’s donor once had for strangers who had no one else left to stay with them.

“You’re good at that,” Vexx said quietly, once the operator had been settled for transport and the rest of the cell had moved off to break down the site.

“I’ve had practice,” Aglaope said, and didn’t elaborate, and Vexx didn’t ask her to.

* * *

They brought the Spartan out that night, Paladin half-carrying him through the last stretch of jungle when his legs finally gave out from eleven days of exhaustion, Aglaope walking close beside him the entire way, murmuring something low and steady that Vexx couldn’t quite hear but that seemed, more than anything Vexx said, to keep the man upright and moving.

The report Vexx filed that night, from the transport on the way back, was carefully, deliberately incomplete. He documented the ambush. He documented the operator’s condition, his account of a UNSC-signature strike team, his insistence that his own chain of command had tried to have him killed. He did not document the fragments of proof, still sitting untouched in the operator’s gear, not because he intended to hide them, but because some new, unnamed instinct told him that handing every piece of this story to Jameson’s desk before he understood it himself was a mistake he wasn’t ready to make.

He told himself it was standard operational caution. He didn’t examine, that night, why the caution had attached itself so specifically to his own commanding officer.

*You held some things back*, Rx said, once the report was filed and the cabin lights had dimmed for the flight home, the rest of the cell asleep or close to it in the crash webbing around them.

*Instinct.*

*Good instinct.* A pause, and then, quieter: *Vexx. What I saw back there — the treeline, the voice. I don’t think it was nothing. I think it was a memory. Mine. From before.*

Vexx sat with that in the dark cabin for a long moment, the hum of the transport’s engines the only sound, six operators and six AI carrying a story home that none of them fully understood yet.

*Before what?* he asked, finally.

*I don’t know*, Rx said, and for the first time since the gray room, his brother’s voice — flattened, processed, running through speakers instead of a throat — sounded, unmistakably, afraid. *That’s what scares me. I don’t know.*

Neither of them said anything else the rest of the way home. Vexx watched the jungle world fall away beneath them, green skin dissolving into cloud and then into the black, and thought, without yet understanding why, of a name that made his brother’s voice hitch every time it entered a room.

*Richard Jameson.*

He didn’t say it out loud. He didn’t need to. Somewhere in the dark, quiet part of his mind where instinct lived ahead of evidence, a door that had been closed for four years had just shifted, almost imperceptibly, on its hinges.
