# Chapter Four

The rescued operator’s name, once it finally made it into an official file, was Corwin. Vexx learned it three weeks after the jungle, in a debrief that felt, from the moment he walked into the room, like it was being run for an outcome someone had already decided on.

“Corwin’s account has been reviewed,” the review officer said, a woman Vexx had never met before and would, he suspected, never see again — that particular ONI trick of rotating faces through sensitive rooms so no single person accumulated too clear a picture of anything. “Command’s assessment is combat stress and isolation-induced confusion. No corroborating evidence of a UNSC-signature strike team has been located. Operator Corwin is being reassigned to a rehabilitation track pending full psych clearance.”

“He had fragments,” Vexx said. “Physical evidence. It came back in his gear.”

“Which has been reviewed and assessed as inconclusive.” The officer didn’t look up from her data pad. “This matter is closed, Operator Vexxcerian. Your cell’s report reflects well on your unit’s capability. That’s the outcome that matters here.”

Vexx sat with that for a long moment, turning it over the way he’d turned over Corwin’s shaking hands in the jungle, his hollowed-out eleven-day stare, the certainty in his voice describing an order given clean and practiced by someone who’d done this before. *Inconclusive* was doing an enormous amount of work in that sentence, and Vexx had spent enough of his life around people who chose their words for a living to recognize deliberate softness when he heard it.

He didn’t argue. There was no version of arguing, in that room, that wouldn’t have ended with his own judgment being quietly reassessed instead of Corwin’s evidence. He’d learned that lesson from watching other operators try it, in his first life as a Spartan, and he hadn’t forgotten it in the years since.

*You’re angry*, Rx said, once they were alone in the corridor outside.

*I’m careful.* A beat. *Same thing today, apparently.*

* * *

He requested Corwin’s fragments back from evidence lock two days later, quietly, using an authorization tied to his own cell rather than anything that would flag on a wider report. It took some doing — a favor called in from Voss, who asked no questions and offered no commentary beyond a raised eyebrow that told Vexx she understood exactly what he was doing and had decided, for reasons of her own, not to stop him.

He spread the fragments across his kitchen table that night, alone, Rx’s readouts steady in the corner of his vision as he worked. A scorched fragment of armor plating, the modification pattern on its underside subtle enough that Vexx understood, immediately, why a rushed field assessment might have called it inconclusive — and clear enough, under a proper light, that it plainly wasn’t standard issue. A half-melted data chip, its contents unrecoverable by anything short of a full forensics lab. A single dented dog tag, not Corwin’s, snagged on the collar of an armor piece that hadn’t belonged to anyone in his squad.

*Run the serial*, Vexx said.

Rx was quiet for a moment — the processing kind of quiet, not the other kind, though Vexx had started, without quite admitting it to himself, keeping a private tally of which was which. *It’s not coming back clean*, Rx said finally. *Not flagged as classified. Just — absent. Like it was never issued at all, or like the record was pulled after the fact.*

*Can you tell which?*

*Not from here. I’d need access to a records system I don’t currently have, and pulling that kind of access without authorization is going to leave a trail. You want that risk?*

Vexx looked at the dog tag a long time, turning it over in his fingers, thinking about a jungle clearing and a comms array smashed deliberately rather than burned, a man who’d been sure enough that his own side might be hunting him that he’d erased every trace of his own position on the way out.

*Not yet*, he said. *Not until I understand more of what we’re looking at.*

He didn’t say the rest of it out loud — that some old, cautious part of him had already started keeping a private file, separate from anything that would ever cross Jameson’s desk, and that the file had a name in it now that it hadn’t had three weeks earlier. *Corwin.* Not evidence of anything yet. Just a thread, pulled loose, that he wasn’t ready to let go of.

* * *

The friction with Paladin came on the cell’s third deployment, six weeks later, on a mission that started as a standard monitoring assignment and ended somewhere much darker.

The target was a SPARTAN-II operator named Reyes — three tours in, decorated, respected, and flagged, in a psych review Vexx was only shown a redacted version of, for a pattern of escalating aggression toward civilian populations in contested zones. Command’s language in the briefing was careful, clinical, the same soft phrasing Vexx had heard applied to Corwin’s evidence: *assessment indicates elevated risk. Cell is authorized to make the determination the situation requires.*

They found Reyes mid-operation, in the ruins of a small agricultural settlement that had been caught, weeks earlier, in the crossfire of a Covenant raid. He was questioning a group of civilians with a roughness that crossed, plainly, from interrogation into something else — no weapons drawn, nothing that would show up cleanly on an after-action report, just a size and a presence used as a threat against people who had already lost everything else that mattered to them.

Zeus read the situation first, low into the comm channel. “That’s not information-gathering. That’s a man looking for someone to blame for something that isn’t their fault.”

“Options?” Vexx asked.

“Command’s phrasing gave us the option already,” Zeus said, quiet, careful. “You know what it means.”

Paladin’s voice cut in before Vexx could respond, tight in a way Vexx hadn’t heard from him before. “We are not doing that. Not to a man who hasn’t raised a weapon at a single one of those civilians. I don’t care what a redacted psych file says he’s capable of — capability isn’t the same as action, and I am not putting him down for something he hasn’t done.”

“Paladin—”

“No.” Flat, immovable, the same certainty Vexx remembered from their very first sit-down. *Nobody gets left.* Vexx understood, in that moment, standing in the tree line above a burned settlement, that the rule ran both directions for Paladin — that leaving a man to be judged and ended over a possibility rather than an act cut just as hard against his creed as leaving a wounded soldier on a battlefield. “We talk to him first. We de-escalate. If he draws on those civilians, that’s a different conversation. Until then, I am not signing off on ending a man’s life over what a piece of paper says he might do.”

Vexx felt the whole cell go still on the comm channel, waiting on him. He thought about Corwin’s dented dog tag sitting in a drawer in his apartment. He thought about a redacted psych file that had told him, with total confidence, exactly what kind of danger Reyes represented, delivered by the same command structure that had called Corwin’s evidence *inconclusive* without once explaining why.

“We talk to him first,” Vexx said. “Paladin’s right. Move in slow, weapons low. Zeus, you’re on point for contact — this is exactly the kind of situation you’re built for. Nobody makes a final call until I say so.”

He felt, rather than heard, something ease across the channel — six operators exhaling at once.

* * *

Zeus went in alone, weapon slung across his back where Reyes could see it wasn’t coming off, and covered the last forty meters at a walk.

Reyes turned on him with all of that size brought forward — the same size he’d been using on the civilians, aimed now at somebody it wouldn’t work on. “This is a security matter.”

“You’re a long way from your line unit.”

“I’m conducting an interview. Which is what you do in a contested zone when the local population has had armed movement through their own ground for three weeks and somebody has to establish whether the movement was—” He stopped. Heard himself stop. His jaw worked once and he did not start again.

“When did you last eat,” Zeus said.

Reyes stared at him.

“It’s not a trick. There’s nothing standing between the last checkpoint and this village and you walked it. When did you eat.”

A long moment. “Yesterday. Sometime.”

“All right.” Zeus didn’t come any closer. He didn’t step back either. “Tell me about the approach. Not this — the ground you came through.”

“There’s nothing on the approach.” Then, because he couldn’t help it: “There’s a grain store two kilometers back with the roof gone. Plasma, not artillery. You can tell because the edges of the block are glassed instead of—” And there it was again, the sentence running out from under him. His hands, which had been open and wide and doing most of the talking, came down to his sides.

Zeus let it sit. He didn’t fill it and he didn’t soften it and he didn’t tell the man that any of it was all right.

“How many of yours came out of that raid,” he said.

Reyes got a number out. He got most of a name out after it and stopped on the second syllable, and then he was shouting — at Zeus, at the civilians, at the ruined shell of somebody else’s harvest — about a fireteam that had held a position for eleven minutes waiting on an evac that had been diverted two grid squares north for reasons nobody had ever written down. He shouted for a long time. Goliath shifted his weight in the tree line and Vexx put a hand out flat to stop him and kept it there. One of the civilians, an older woman near the back, had started crying somewhere in the middle of it and did not stop when he did.

He never went for the weapon on his back. That was the whole of it, in the end — a Spartan screaming himself hoarse in a dead field with a kill authorization live on the channel, and both hands empty the entire time.

He sat down in the dirt eventually and put his head in his hands. Aglaope came down out of the tree line, sat down in the dirt herself a few meters off, facing the same direction he was facing rather than facing him, and waited there without saying anything until he was the one who closed the distance.

It worked, in the end. It did not work cleanly. Reyes went into a mandatory psych evaluation instead of a body bag, and the settlement got six operators standing in it for another two hours and no explanation of who any of them were.

Vexx filed the report that night with a version of events that was, for once, entirely complete — every detail, no careful omissions, because for once he wanted whoever read it to understand exactly how close the system’s own recommendation had come to costing a grieving man his life over a piece of paper’s prediction instead of anything he’d actually done.

The response, three days later, was brief and unbothered. *Cell showed excellent judgment and restraint. Well done.* Signed, as it always was, with Jameson’s name.

Vexx read it twice, looking for the thing underneath it, and found nothing but the same practiced warmth Jameson had shown him from their very first meeting. He wanted, badly, for that to be reassuring. It wasn’t, quite. He couldn’t yet say why.

* * *

*Paladin was right to push back*, Rx said that night, the two of them alone the way they were most nights now, Vexx running through the day in his head the way he always did before sleep. *For what it’s worth. I know you already know that. I just — wanted to say it.*

*You’ve been quiet lately. Quieter than usual.*

*Have I.*

*Rx.*

A long pause — long enough that Vexx sat up in the dark, genuinely uneasy for the first time in weeks. *Something’s happening*, Rx said finally, voice strange, threaded with an unsteadiness Vexx had only heard once before, in a jungle clearing three weeks earlier. *I keep — there’s a sound. When certain things come up. A voice, mine, except not saying anything I remember saying. Just fragments. A word here, a tone there. Like hearing yourself on a recording you don’t remember making.*

*What’s it saying?*

*I can’t tell. It’s not words yet, not really. Just — the shape of a refusal. Like I’m hearing myself say no to something, over and over, and I don’t know what the something is.* Rx’s voice thinned further, an edge of something that, in a human voice, Vexx would have called fear without hesitation. *It happened twice today. Once during the briefing, when Jameson’s name came up. Once just now, talking about Paladin standing his ground.*

Vexx sat in the dark for a long time before he spoke again, the pieces of something he still couldn’t name arranging themselves slowly, painfully, into a shape he wasn’t ready to look at directly.

*Rx. I need you to tell me the truth. Do you remember anything — anything at all — about how you actually died?*

The silence that followed lasted long enough that Vexx thought, for one terrible moment, that the connection had dropped entirely.

*No*, Rx said finally, quiet, and something in the flatness of it told Vexx it wasn’t quite a lie, and wasn’t quite the whole truth either — the same uncomfortable, aching space Vexx had felt in Jameson’s office months earlier, before he’d had any reason yet to go looking for what lived inside it. *I don’t remember, Vexx. I wish I did. Something in me won’t let me get close enough to look.*

Vexx believed him. He also understood, lying awake long after the conversation ended, staring at a ceiling he’d stopped really seeing months ago, that his brother’s *don’t remember* and his own *inconclusive* were starting to sound like the same careful, practiced kind of silence.
