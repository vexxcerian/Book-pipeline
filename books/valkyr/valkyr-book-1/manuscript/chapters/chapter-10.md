Eleven forty, room 2-14, north end of the operations block — nothing on the summons about what for.

It had come up on the pad at twenty past ten with no name attached to it: routing block, room, time, the word ATTEND set in capitals, a clause reference that Vexx looked up on the walk down, understood no better for having looked, then carried into the stairwell with him because it pointed at a schedule sitting two authorizations above anything he held. He had assumed Merrick. That was the shape a summons had in his head now, a fortnight after a transport had gone out with a man on it repeating himself, and he had put together most of an answer about the assessment before the answer turned out to be for a question nobody was going to ask him. The corridor at that end had been re-floored in the summer, the new covering stopping a couple of paces short of the fire door — where somebody had run out of it, or run out of the money for it, leaving a lip you could catch a boot on.

*It’s an evidence-handling schedule*, Rx said. *The one about who’s allowed to carry what out of a locked room. You’d think a man would know that already.*

*Am I allowed a representative?*

*You get one if it’s a board. It won’t be a board. A board is three people, a table you can’t reach across, and a stenographer who hates you personally.*

The room had been a store once, converted by somebody working to a budget that ran to a table, four chairs, the paint. You could see where the shelving had come off the long wall — a double row of bolt holes filled with something a shade paler than the paint — the vent above the door painted over with the grille shut, then cut open again later with a knife, by somebody in a hurry. A table. Four chairs, two of them pushed in. A jug of water, two glasses on a tray, put out for a thing expected to take a while, the tray gone brown at the rim like something through a machine wash too often.

She was at the far side of the table, writing, and she finished the line before she looked up — not making a point of it, not the small held pause of a person who means you to wait, simply finishing a line.

The notebook was paper. Vexx had not seen paper in that building outside of the laminated card taped to the terminal down in the annex — this was a bound book, cloth spine, a corner gone soft like a much-handled card, lying open under her left hand while her right went on at a steady unhurried rate, the pen making a sound in the quiet room like a fingernail going down a seam.

“Operator Vexxcerian. Dessen, Internal Compliance.” She capped the pen, set it in the gutter of the book. “Sit wherever you like. It’ll be about an hour.”

He sat.

“Before I ask you anything, you’re entitled to hear the finding as it currently stands. Some people would rather not. You can tell me now if you’d rather not.”

“I’d rather hear it.”

“Most do.” She turned the notebook back through itself without hunting for the place, and read.

*Material recovered at the Corwin site was released from evidence lock on an authorization raised inside the assessing cell, countersigned by the officer commanding that cell, for the stated purpose of secondary examination. It was not returned within the period the release specified — the chain of custody is broken at the point of release, and has not since been re-established.*

She let it sit exactly as long as it needed, no longer.

“That’s two sentences,” she said, “and I’ve been three weeks on them. I’d like to go through how you’d amend either.”

* * *

She began where they teach you to begin, without any of the small theater Vexx had been braced for — no folder pushed across the table, no pause held for effect, no question asked twice over in a different tone to see whether the answer moved. She asked him what the material was, and he told her: armor plating with a modification pattern on the underside, a data chip burned past recovery, a dog tag. She asked what the review board had ruled. He told her that too — she wrote it down without saying what she thought of it, without any of it reaching her face at all.

“You raised the release yourself.”

“I did.”

“And countersigned it.”

“Yes.”

“Did anybody in the chain of that transaction work outside your cell.”

“The lock clerk processed it.”

“Name.”

“I don’t remember.”

That came out level, easy, a shade ahead of the question — and went down in the book without comment, because it was the truth about a thing he was choosing not to say, and there is no strain in that at all. He had been withholding for thirty years, from review boards, from a chaplain, from two officers in dress uniform standing in his doorway with a folder they did not open. It cost him nothing. It never had.

“The clerk isn’t my question,” Dessen said. “The clerk processed a release that was in order on its face. I’ll put it down that you don’t recall, because you don’t, and if it turns out later that you do, that’ll be a different conversation, a worse one.” She drew a short line under something. “The water’s been standing since yesterday. I’d have it anyway.”

The water in the jug had been there since before the room was booked and he drank two glasses of it.

* * *

“What was the material required for.”

“Secondary examination.”

She wrote that down, all of it, in about the time it takes to say it.

“What was the material used for.”

Vexx heard the change, could not, sitting there, work out what it bought her. “I looked at it again,” he said. “Under a better light than the one in the assessment bay. The modification on the plate isn’t visible under a field lamp. It’s visible on a kitchen table with a lamp brought down close to it.”

Her pen went on a good deal longer that time, and when it stopped she read back what she had put down, moving nothing but her eyes.

“What is the material being used for.”

The vent over the door ticked as the heating came up, small reports out of the metal at uneven intervals, like a kettle beginning to think about it.

He had known the shape of the third before she got to the end of it — had nothing standing behind it either, because the honest answer was a drawer beside a bed in an apartment with boxes still taped shut in the corner of the front room, and any other answer had to be built out of nothing, on the spot, in front of a woman who wrote things down.

“It isn’t,” he said. “Being used.”

“It isn’t being used.”

“No.”

She wrote the first answer, the second, the third, each under the last, drawing no line between them, not looking up while she did it — three separate facts about the same man, small and upright on the page like items on a shopping list, standing in her book afterward exactly as they had come out of him.

*She writes at about ninety words a minute*, Rx said, *which for longhand is not human, and I have been watching her do it for twenty-two minutes and I cannot see her lift the pen at the ends of the words.* A pause. *There’s a property schedule as well as an evidence schedule. Different remit, different penalty. I’m reading it now in case anybody wants it.*

Nobody wanted it.

* * *

Trays going down a steel rail. The doors at both ends propped, the heat off the servery meeting the cold coming in at them, men eating in their hundreds under a low ceiling, the light arriving off to one side — long, the color it goes late on. He had been putting that at eleven hundred for as long as he had been telling it to himself. It was not eleven hundred. It might have been the late sitting, the one you drew when you were on the ranges all morning, in which case everything after it in that day sat in the wrong order too.

There is a way of folding a shirt so the collar never creases, and neither of them had ever learned it. Twenty years of doing it wrong, side by side, in the same room, out of the same locker.

“—the fourteen days,” Dessen said. “Do you dispute the period.”

“No.”

“Do you dispute that it ran out.”

“No.”

“Then help me with the middle of it.” She read back over the last page with the flat of her thumb holding it down. “Fourteen days is a long window. It’s long because the people who wrote the schedule expected examination to take time. What I don’t have is anything between the release and today. No extension. No note to the lock. No line anywhere saying a man was still working.”

He said, “I logged an extension.”

It was out of him before he had decided anything — the first lie he had told in that room, a manufactured one, a different animal altogether from a withheld one, and his body knew the difference about a second before his mouth caught up with it.

“Against which reference.”

Nothing came. There was a reference format — he had seen it a hundred times, a letter, four figures, a stroke, the year — and he could see the shape of the box it went in on the form, could not put anything at all inside the box.

“Where would I find it,” Dessen said, without any edge on it.

“You wouldn’t.” His hand was on the edge of the table. He took it off. “I didn’t log an extension. I said that because you asked me a question I had no answer to, and it seemed better to have one.”

“Thank you.” She wrote it. “That’s the sort of thing that gets people put in front of a board. The reason it isn’t going to today is that you took it back inside a minute, and I’d like that on the page as well.” The pen stopped. She looked at it, clicked it twice, put it down, took a second out of the spine of the book. “Every last one of these dies at the tail. Every single one. They’re bought by the gross.”

She wrote three more lines with the new pen, and the sound of it was different, wetter, laying the ink down like a brush instead of scratching it in. Vexx sat in a chair that was too low and did not adjust it.

* * *

“I want to be sure you understand what the finding is about,” Dessen said, “because most people I sit with think it’s about the rules, and it isn’t.”

“Go on.”

“Say the board reopens Corwin. Say somebody upstairs reads it again in a year and thinks better of it, and sends for the plate with the modification on the underside.” She turned a leaf. “It will be produced. It will be in good order. And the first thing any competent officer asks is where it has been, and the answer is a kitchen table, in a residence, under a lamp, alone, for the better part of a year. That plate cannot now be put in front of anybody. Not because it’s contaminated — because there’s no longer anyone who can say it isn’t.”

The heating went off.

“You didn’t destroy it,” she said. “You did something narrower. You made it useless to him.”

Vexx looked at the far wall, at the paler filler in the bolt holes, at the grille painted shut, then cut open by a man who had needed air more than he had needed the paintwork. He had gone down to that counter on a Thursday afternoon with an authorization on a pad. He had read the second field of the form, and had read it properly, and had signed underneath it. He had been careful. He had been careful the entire time.

“Yes,” he said.

“I’m not saying it to make you feel it.” She said this without any softening on it whatsoever, like a woman reading out a train time. “I’m saying it because the finding goes forward as read, and attached to it is a recommendation. I’ll read you that too — you’ll hear it eventually, and it’s better heard here. Recommendation is that the operator’s independent evidentiary authority be placed under review, and that the review consider his fitness to hold—”

The door opened.

* * *

Jameson had a data pad in one hand, his cap in the other, and he had come from outside — the shoulders of his uniform dark with it, weather standing in the corridor behind him, the smell of rain on wool arriving in the room about a second after he did.

He did not sit. He did not say excuse me. He stood inside the door with the pad down at his side, letting the end of Dessen’s sentence finish itself in the air, and for about a second nothing at all happened.

“Say the rest of it.”

“Officer Dessen, Internal Compliance. And you are—”

“I know who you are. I’ve read your file. I’ve read your finding. I came from the other end of the building without a coat. Say the rest of the sentence.”

“—his fitness to hold operational command.”

“There it is.” He set the pad down on the table without looking at where it landed. “Do you know what that word does. Not to a career — to a file. It sits in the second field for the rest of a man’s service, every board that ever opens him reads it first, and not a single one of them ever finds out it was written by somebody who has never in her life had to decide anything in under a minute. Compliance has produced four findings in this building this year. Three of them were about parking. You have a room, a jug of water, three weeks — and a man who did the only honest thing available to him in a corridor at the end of a very bad month, and you have found a means of making that the problem, because the actual problem is a review board, and boards are not in your remit, and they have taken care to see that they never will be.”

It went on for about eight seconds.

Vexx came half out of the chair without having worked out what he intended to do with the rest of the movement, and sat back down into it.

Then it stopped, like a machine stopped by somebody taking the power off it rather than switching it, and Jameson stood there with his cap in his hand, his mouth shut, and something went across his face Vexx had not seen on it in two years.

“No,” he said. “No, that was contemptible.”

Dessen had not moved. She had not put the pen down either, and the hand holding it had stayed where it was on the page, at the end of an unfinished word.

“I said your work was trivial. It isn’t. It’s the only reason a file in this building means anything at all — I have relied on it twice this year, both times to my advantage, neither time with a note of thanks to anybody.” He was looking at her, not at the table, not at Vexx. “The thing about the parking, I don’t know to be true. I said it because I wanted it to land, because it’s the sort of thing that lands. That’s worse than the first part. The first part was temper. The second part I chose.”

He stopped, and then, because he had evidently decided the account was not yet complete:

“And I came in over the top of a sentence you were entitled to finish, in front of an operator under your questioning, which will have done your authority in this room considerable damage. I’d like to say I’ll repair it. I can’t — there isn’t a mechanism. I’m sorry. All four things.”

Dessen wrote it down.

She wrote down the apology at the same speed she had written down the insult, in the same hand, without hurrying, without any change at all in the sound of the pen — the only thing in that room, from first to last, that treated the two of them as the same size.

“That isn’t a matter for the finding,” she said, “so it goes in the margin. Do you want to sit down?”

“Thank you. I would.”

* * *

He sat where the chairs were pushed in — pulling one out, turning it, doing none of the small business a man does to make a point of arriving late.

“Read me the schedule you’re citing. Not the clause number. The words.”

“All of it?”

“All of it.”

She read them.

“Right.” Jameson put his cap on his knee. “Then let me put your case, because I don’t think you’ve put it hard enough, and I’d rather we all argued with the strong version. It isn’t that material walked out of a lock. Material walks out of locks in this building forty times a week, comes back, nobody writes anything down. It’s that at no point in this transaction was there a person who was not him. He raised it. He countersigned it. And” — the pad came up, went down again — “he drew it in person, off the counter, himself, which isn’t in your finding and should be, because that was the last place a third party could have got into the chain, and he shut that door too. Not deliberately. That’s what makes it worse. A man doing it deliberately would have sent a runner for appearances. He didn’t think of appearances at all, which tells you he wasn’t thinking about the record, which is precisely the fault you’re describing.”

Dessen was writing.

“So it isn’t a custody problem,” Jameson said. “It’s a solitude problem. You’re saying he was alone in it start to finish, with nothing in the file that could have stopped him.”

“Yes.” She underlined something. “That’s better than I had it.”

“Good.” He set his hands on the arms of the chair. “Now. What did the board rule.”

“Inconclusive.”

“What did the board rule about the material.”

Nothing in the room for a second.

“That no corroborating evidence had been located,” Dessen said.

“Read the disposition line at the bottom of the ruling. Not the summary — the disposition.”

She had it up on her own pad inside a minute. She read it. Then she read it again, with her lips moving and her pen not going, the second reading taking about three times as long as the first.

“Struck from the evidentiary record,” she said. “Retained as recovered property pending disposal.”

“Three weeks before he signed anything.” Jameson said it with no lift on it whatsoever, like a man reporting a figure off a gauge. “The chain of custody you’re describing was ended in writing by a review board on a Tuesday afternoon. Everything that came out of that lock afterward came out as property. You can find him for a property return that’s overdue since the summer — you should, because it is. That’s a real finding, I won’t stand in front of it. But it’s a paragraph, a signature, a line in the quarterly. It isn’t a fitness review, because there was no chain left for him to break by the time he got there. They broke it. He inherited it.”

Dessen did not answer for some time, and the silence she kept was a working one — she went back through her own pad, then through the notebook, then through the pad again, taking long enough over it that the heating came on, ran, went off.

“The schedule doesn’t distinguish,” she said at last.

“No. It should.”

“It doesn’t, I’d have to argue the distinction, I’d win it, it would take four months.” She capped the pen. “The finding stands. It’s correct, I’m not amending it. The recommendation falls away — I’ll enter the disposition line, and the reason. You’ll get a property return notice inside a fortnight. Answer it the same day.”

“I will,” Vexx said.

“People say that.”

Jameson stood, picked his cap up off his knee, stopped halfway to the door with the pad under his arm.

“Pressure’s fallen nine points since Tuesday,” he said. “Which is not weather. That’s a front. It’ll be in off the water by tonight.”

Dessen turned a leaf and went on writing.

He went out, left the door standing open, and somebody had to get up to shut it. It was Vexx.

* * *

For a while it was only the pen. Dessen wrote, and Vexx stood by the door with his hand still on it, looking down a corridor with nothing in it, listening to the elevator at the far end make the noise an elevator makes when it is going somewhere without you.

“Sit down a minute,” she said. “I have to read it to you again as it goes in. You have to hear it. Then we’re finished.”

She turned back through the book.

*Material recovered at the Corwin site was released from evidence lock on an authorization raised inside the assessing cell, countersigned by the officer commanding that cell, for the stated purpose of secondary examination. It was not returned within the period the release specified — the chain of custody is broken at the point of release, and has not since been re-established.*

Every word of it true. Entered, finished with, filed where nobody would have cause to open it again — and the tag with the dent in one corner, the serial that came back as nothing at all, lying in a drawer beside his bed among a watch strap, a cufflink, a torch he never used, half a mile east of the chair he was sitting in, where it would still be tonight.

“That’s it,” she said.

He said thank you. It came out of him with more in it than he had meant to put there, and she took no notice of it at all.

She was packing the notebook away — a case with a strap, the strap set for somebody else’s shoulder, never since changed — and stopped with it half in.

“Do you know anybody in operational support,” she said.

“No.”

“No.” She got the case shut. “They grade the transfers on a board. The board sits twice a year. I’ve been told both times that my experience is very valuable where it is.”

Vexx said something about it being a hard year for postings — true, useless, the sort of remark he would have been annoyed to be handed himself.

“It is,” Dessen said, in the voice of a woman filing something.

* * *

The elevator at the north end takes its time, and Jameson was still standing at it when Vexx came out of the room, pad under one arm, reading something off it with his thumb.

*He’ll have gone by now*, Rx had said on the stairs, answering something Vexx had not asked him, and had said almost nothing since the audit-room door opened.

“There you are.” Jameson turned his cap over once. “That woman is going to run this directorate in nine years, and I have made an enemy of her in a store cupboard.”

“Sir—”

“Richard.”

“Richard.” The word came awkwardly out of a mouth trained out of it in the first month of a life he had left thirty years ago. “I wanted to say thank you. Before you go. I’d have signed anything she put in front of me by the end of that hour.”

“You’d have signed the truth. That’s the trouble with an honest man in a compliance room — he’s the only person in it who can be talked out of his own position.” Jameson pressed the call plate a second time, which does nothing to an elevator anywhere in the galaxy. “Don’t thank me. I read a disposition line. That isn’t a favor, it’s Thursday.”

There it was, under the breastbone, the same as it had been in a briefing room two levels below anything with a name on the door, the first time a man he had never met said his brother’s name out loud and got the weight of it right. Four years of an unopened folder, a chaplain, a box with nothing in it — and this was the second time in his life anybody had gone and stood in front of a thing for him without being asked to.

He had his mouth open. He shut it. Then he did it again, which is a thing a man cannot do twice in front of another man without it being noticed. Jameson turned toward him and gave him his whole attention, and waited.

“My brother’s—”

Three words, and then whatever came after them did not arrive.

He did not fill it. He did not look away. He did none of the small courteous things people do to let a man off — he stood with his cap in both hands, his whole attention on Vexx’s face, nothing on his own but the ordinary concern of somebody who has noticed that the person in front of him is having difficulty, and he waited a good deal longer than the silence could comfortably carry.

“It’ll keep,” Vexx said.

“All right.”

That was all. He did not ask again. He did not tilt his head, or say *are you sure*, or leave a door propped open in his voice for it to come back through later. He let it go, entirely, the way a decent man does — and stood there with him, in a corridor, in front of an elevator that was not coming, saying nothing.

Then, because the silence had gone on long enough and one of them had to spend it:

“The gauge in my quarters is mechanical. Aneroid. My father’s. It reads light, and I’ve never had it set, because if I have it set it stops being his — there’s a trick to reading a falling glass, you don’t read the number, you read how long it took to get there, and if it goes in under six hours what you want is your windows shut, everything off the—”

The elevator came. He got into it, still talking, and the doors took the rest of the sentence.

* * *

Vexx stood there a while afterward with his hand not quite down from where it had been, held a little out from his side like a man carrying something wet.

*The station pressure*, he said. *Can you get it?*

*It’s on the met page. It’s been on the met page every day for four years, along with first light, last light, the state of the water and a fishing advisory nobody in this building has ever needed.* A pause with nothing in it. *Why?*

*Read me the week.*

Rx read him the week. Vexx took the figures and put them where he used to put a callsign, a grid, a departure window — into the place built into him at fourteen for holding things he would have to say back quickly, correctly, under pressure, to a man who would be pleased with him — then stood in the corridor at the north end of the operations block, with the new floor covering stopping short of the fire door where the money had run out, and learned them.
